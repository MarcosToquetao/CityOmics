## Abstract

Which components of a place host which crimes, and when, is hard to measure where police records lack coordinates. We read São Paulo as a spatially resolved tissue: 1.65 million fiscal parcels are cells, census tracts are units of known composition and 0.69 million police records cover 15 crime types. A composition-aware model gives classes of cell distinct crime profiles that survive district-level risk and are consistent with geocoded events at finer scale (median Spearman ρ = 0.78; 0.76 with disjoint halves). Daily rhythms of phone theft and robbery differ by class and replicate across years, and robbery risk per pedestrian is highest at night. Persistent differences between places that composition does not explain account for at least as much of how much crime a tract records. Month-to-month variation within tracts exceeds sampling noise mainly where crime is highest, and the forecasting models tested captured little of it.

<!--MAIN-->
Crime is concentrated at a small share of addresses and street segments, and the same places tend to stay at the top for years{sherman1989,weisburd2004,weisburd2015}. The pattern holds in Latin American cities{chainey2019,demelo2015}. Its explanations concern what places contain and when people use them: routine activities bring offenders and targets together where capable guardians are absent{cohen1979,hollispeel2011}, some land uses generate or attract crime{brantingham1995,bernasco2011} and a few facilities of each type account for most of its crime{eck2007}. Facility and land-use studies relate crime to uses as covariates, mostly for a few uses or crime types at a time{stucky2009,twinam2017}, risk terrain models combine many layers into a single risk surface{caplan2011}, and evidence on whether the effect of a place changes with the hour or season is mixed{haberman2015,bernasco2017,vansleeuwen2021,quick2019}. Two gaps remain. Few studies read the full composition of a city, all its crime types and the hour together. And in many cities police records lack usable coordinates: in São Paulo, 17% of phone crime does, rising to 81% on the rail network, as do 81% of residential thefts and robberies, so the contribution of each component must be inferred from areal counts, the problem of ecological inference{robinson1950,goodman1953}.

Spatial transcriptomics faces the same problem. Its measurement units, called spots, capture mixtures of cells{moses2022}. Given reference profiles of cell types, deconvolution estimates the composition of each spot{kleshchevnikov2022,cable2022}. When composition is known, the same model run the other way estimates the profile of each type{shenorr2010,newman2019}. Around this core the field measures profiles over many outputs at once{kotliar2019}, rhythms that differ between tissues{zhang2014}, cell states beyond type and the sampling-noise floor of sparse counts{sarkar2021}. Urban research has borrowed from biology before, from the human ecology of the Chicago school{park1925,shaw1942} and the DNA of neighbourhoods{delmelle2016}, mostly as a framing.

Here we use this toolkit as an instrument on São Paulo, a city of 11.5 million people. Fiscal parcels are cells, census tracts are spots of known composition and crime is a phenotype measured over the tissue (Fig. 1a). We ask three questions: which crimes each class of cell hosts, at what hour, and what remains to be predicted once composition and history are known. Parcel types were defined without crime data (Methods), and all analyses of the atlas use 77 exploration districts, holding out 19 districts for a single confirmatory test (Methods). We find that composition structures which crimes a place records and when, that persistent differences between places beyond composition account for more of how much crime it records, and that the forecasting models we tested capture little of the month-to-month variation within tracts.

## Results

### A city read as a tissue

We described each of 1,651,813 fiscal parcels by 30 attributes from the 2025 property-tax cadastre and the business registry, covering land use, building standard and height, frontage and the activities of businesses linked to the parcel by address. Clustering these attributes without crime data (Methods) gave 15 parcel types, and buildings with at least four floors or eight units, which the clustering did not separate, formed a sixteenth type by rule. The types were stable across subsamples (adjusted Rand index (ARI) = 0.98) and agreed weakly with a geodemographic k-means baseline (ARI = 0.40; Fig. 1b and Fig. 2a,b,f). Residential types differ by building standard and by whether the parcel hosts a registered business, often a home-based micro-entrepreneur; commerce appears as mixed residential and commercial types, and the rarer commercial, service and vacant-corner types were the least stable (Supplementary Methods). Tracts mix many types, which are organized into neighbourhoods and contiguous domains (Fig. 2c–e and Extended Data Fig. 1). With favelas (3,879 census areas whose informal homes are absent from the cadastre), parcels lacking a cadastral record, three classes of street, rail stations and bus terminals, the 16 types form 23 cell classes (glossary in Methods). The composition of a tract in these classes is its tissue.

### Each cell class has a crime profile

We modelled the crime count of each tract as the sum of what is attributed to its cells: a Poisson model whose expectation is Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~, where E~sk~ is the exposure of class k in tract s (metres of frontage or of street, or number of stations) and λ~k~ is the rate of the class (Methods). This is Poisson ecological regression with known exposures{goodman1953}, the urban counterpart of estimating type-specific profiles from mixtures of known composition{shenorr2010}. For 121,434 phone crimes (thefts and robberies of mobile phones) in exploration tracts in 2025, composition alone explained 44% of the deviance relative to a constant count per tract (D² = 0.44; Fig. 3a). Per km of frontage, rates were highest on mid- and high-rise buildings (37 events per year, 95% interval 19–56) and mixed residential and commercial parcels (13–16), and close to zero on low-rise houses; arterial streets recorded 16 events per km of street, local streets 0.8, and rail stations 73 per station (39–109; 36 with geocoded events only). Among per-km classes whose intervals exclude zero, rates differ by two orders of magnitude. A multiplicative risk level per district preserved the broad order of classes (Spearman ρ = 0.96) but changed the top class, lowering central rates to 15 per km on high-rise buildings and 36 per station. Simulations with known rates recovered the order of classes (median ρ = 0.95; 0.84 under district-level risk) but, under district-level risk, neither absolute rates (median error 33%) nor interval coverage, so we interpret orders and shares (Extended Data Fig. 10a). Re-allocating the 100,347 geocoded events to the nearest street segment and its abutting parcels reproduced the ranking of cell classes (ρ = 0.83; 0.77 among parcel types), a consistency check at finer scale that held when one half of the geocoded events was used for fitting and the other for checking (ρ = 0.83; 0.77 among parcel types; Extended Data Fig. 10b).

We then extended the outcome to 15 crime types (0.69 million events in 2024–2025; Extended Data Fig. 2a) and fitted each with the same exposures. The resulting class-by-crime profiles behave like reference signatures (Fig. 3b): the most enriched class differs between crime types, with 10 distinct classes at the top. Lethal violence, 66% geocoded, is enriched on low-standard houses, vacant land and favelas (log₂ enrichment +1.8, +1.9 and +1.4; low-standard types pooled), cargo theft and robbery on the same houses and favelas (+1.7 and +2.0), vehicle theft on houses with wholesale businesses (+2.1), vehicle robbery on low-standard houses (+1.6) and phone theft at rail stations (+1.7; not tested at finer scale). Crime types defined by place behave as positive controls, the residential one resting mostly on reconciled events: theft and robbery on public transport are enriched at bus terminals and rail stations (+2.7 and +2.5), residential theft and robbery on middle-standard houses (+3.8), and traffic casualties have their highest rate among streets on arterials (4.5 against 0.2 per km on local streets). The profiles survive district-level risk (median ρ = 0.92 between shares with and without district effects; same top class for 11 of 15 types). The finer-scale check held in the 12 crime types with at least 80% of events geocoded (median ρ = 0.78, from 0.44 for cargo theft and robbery to 0.87 for vehicle theft; Fig. 3c). Factorizing the tract-by-crime matrix{kotliar2019} gave four programs, stable across seeds, that read as transport, dense-centre, peripheral-logistics and peripheral-residential crime (Fig. 3d,e).

### Each class keeps its own daily rhythm

Classes also differ in when crime occurs (Fig. 4b). We call this the tissue clock: a class-specific daily profile, not an endogenous oscillator. We compared the departure of each class's period-of-day profile from the city's with a null that permutes periods within districts. For phone theft, the pre-specified test, class-specific departures exceeded the null in 2024 and 2025 (P ≤ 0.005 in each year) and replicated between years (r = 0.96; Fig. 4a). So did phone robbery (P ≤ 0.005 in each year; r = 0.94). Both held when whole blocks of events of the same tract, period and day or month were permuted (P ≤ 0.002); other street robbery also replicated (r = 0.77) and traffic casualties did not (r = 0.08). Robbery on mixed, high-rise and service parcels peaks between 20:00 and 22:00, on arterials at 19:00 and at rail stations at 18:00, whereas the model, for classes with little phone crime, places the peak on low-standard houses and favelas before dawn (Fig. 4b). A model-free check supports the direction at half the magnitude: in the 2,379 tracts where low-standard houses make up most of the frontage, 19% of weekday phone robberies with an exact hour occurred between 04:00 and 06:00 (95% interval 16–22%), against 5% in tracts dominated by commerce and 10% citywide; we treat this as exploratory.

A rhythm of this kind could simply track the number of people outside. We estimated the hourly ambient population{hipp2019} of every tract from the 2023 Origin–Destination household survey, separating people present from people walking on the street (Methods). The ordering of classes changes when rates are divided by pedestrians (Spearman ρ = 0.64, 95% interval 0.51–0.69, against rates per km; Fig. 4f), although pedestrian density varies across classes far less than crime rates (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Industrial parcels, with few pedestrians, rise 12 positions in the per-pedestrian ranking, as crime attractors do, while high-rise buildings fall 8, as generators do{brantingham1995,bernasco2011}. The rhythm does not follow the crowd (Fig. 4c). Across classes, the median correlation between the hourly profiles of robbery and of pedestrians is 0.05 (0.03–0.07), and between 00:00 and 05:59 citywide robbery risk per pedestrian is 4.6 times its daily mean (4.2–5.1; Fig. 4d), with the 2017 survey giving the same picture (Methods). Robbery on local and arterial streets is less frequent per day at weekends than on weekdays (0.77, 0.64–0.88, and 0.79, 0.60–0.98), when fewer people commute, and on high-rise buildings more frequent (1.35, 1.03–1.74; Fig. 4e). These patterns fit low guardianship at night and offenders converging on commuters' paths{cohen1979}, but darkness{tompson2013} and offender routines remain untested alternatives.

### Type and state: a persistent effect of place

Composition is not all that a tract carries (Fig. 5). Out of sample, tissue features without crime history predicted the 2025 tract rate with R² = 0.27 (0.32 with tract covariates), against 0.48 for the tract's historical mean (0.39 with integer imputation of events without coordinates, whose expected allocation follows past events; Fig. 5a). Parcel types (R² = 0.22) did not outperform the same attributes without typing (0.26), so typing serves attribution and interpretation rather than prediction. Splitting the between-tract variance of log annual rates over 2022–2025, composition accounted for 42% and a component that persists across years beyond composition for 46%, with a cross-term of −11% for their covariance; in the 3,145 tracts with at least 10 expected events per year, the split was 23% against 60% (Fig. 5b). The persistent component held with geocoded events only (40% against 44%) and with integer imputations (41% against 45%), and residuals remained correlated at a lag of three years (r = 0.63; Fig. 5c). The tissue itself barely changes: 97.5% of parcels keep their family from one year to the next in the 2012–2025 cadastres (Fig. 5d), an upper bound given classifier error.

Statistically, this component is persistent heterogeneity that observed composition does not explain, and covariances across years cannot separate it from state dependence{nagin2000}. In the omics analogy it plays the role of a cell state beyond type, a label for what typing misses rather than an identified mechanism. Candidates include uses the cadastre misses (street vending, informal commerce, unlinked businesses), place management{eck2007} and territorial control by organized crime{biderman2019}. Typing describes what a place is. Where crime concentrates most, its history says more.

### Rates move within a stable tissue

Phone crime fell by 20% between 2022 and 2025 in exploration tracts (Fig. 5e), through robbery (−40%) rather than theft (−2%). With composition fixed at 2025, we compared class rates with the city's within each crime, because classes differ in their mix of theft and robbery. Phone robbery fell faster than the city's robbery on residential parcel types together (relative ratio 0.27, 95% interval 0.05–0.64) and, in exploration, slower at hubs, which comprise commercial and service classes, stations and terminals (1.18, 1.04–1.32), on arterial streets (1.21, 1.02–1.46) and in favelas (1.23, 1.01–1.56); stations and terminals (0.92, 0.71–1.23) and local streets (0.96, 0.83–1.13) kept pace with the city. In absolute terms the fall tracked where robbery had been: hub-dominated tracts held and lost 20% of it. Candidate drivers include the national phone-blocking scheme launched in December 2023{celularseguro}, policing and changes in reporting, none of which we test. Other crime types moved in both directions (Fig. 5f). Openings and closures of bars and restaurants and openings of shops on street segments were followed by no detectable change in street crime over 12 months (95% intervals within −7% to +8% of the baseline rate in all six variants; Extended Data Fig. 3).

### Month-to-month variation is concentrated and poorly forecast

Flagging the 5% of the area at highest forecast risk, an ensemble of forecasting models captured 53% of 2025 phone crime, as much as the historical mean (52%) and the share expected if the ensemble's forecasts were exact (52%), against 65% for an ex-post oracle (Fig. 6a). We compared explained variance with a noise ceiling, the largest R² a forecast could reach if monthly counts were Poisson around their expectation (Methods); it bounds what any forecast could explain, not what the available data can. Between and within tracts together, the ensemble reached R² = 0.80 of a ceiling of 0.92 for tract-months, and 87–99% of the ceiling at every scale (Fig. 6b). Within tracts, it explained R² = 0.018 of a ceiling of 0.56 (0.58 when events without coordinates were imputed as integer counts).

The within-tract ceiling comes almost entirely from the 1% of tracts with most crime, where it reaches 0.78 and the ensemble explains 0.03; in the other 99%, which record two-thirds of phone crime, it is 0.08–0.19. A per-tract dispersion test rejected Poisson noise in 3.9% of tracts and in 14% of tracts with at least one event per month, against 1.7% and 2.2% expected under a null simulated through the same reconciliation (Fig. 6c and Extended Data Fig. 10d). A common relative overdispersion reproduces the concentration of extra-Poisson variance in these tracts (88% expected, 86% observed). Month-to-month variation is thus consistent with a property shared by all tracts that only high-count tracts make visible, much as Poisson noise masks variation in expression at low counts in single cells{sarkar2021} and sparse counts bias measures of crime concentration{bernascosteenbeek2017}. Reconciliation smooths monthly counts: hiding the coordinates of geocoded events at each stratum's observed missing rate and reconciling them recovered tract-month counts (r = 0.99) but removed 13% of within-tract variance (35% when missingness rose with tract crime), so ceilings and rejection rates of reconciled counts are conservative (Extended Data Fig. 10c). These statements concern phone crime at the scale of tract-months. Near-repeat excitation over days and hundreds of metres{johnson2007} (Extended Data Fig. 9a) averages out within a month and a tract.

Forecasting methods from seismology, epidemiology, ecology, econometrics and machine learning did little better than simple baselines. In a benchmark on 2025, run in development, the stacked ensemble improved the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS; Methods) over a negative binomial generalized linear model by 6% (difference −0.016, 95% interval −0.023 to −0.011) and the best single model, gradient boosting on core covariates, did so by 5% (Fig. 6d; Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5), but no model significantly raised the share of crime captured in 1% of the area. Larger gains reported elsewhere come at finer scales or with other metrics{wu2022,alborszumel2026}. Forecasts were calibrated within 5% across tract income quintiles, yet flagging by density placed 36% of the flagged area in the richest quintile and 3% in the poorest, which recorded 26% and 7% of phone crime (Fig. 6e). For lethal violence at district scale, no model improved on the negative binomial baseline (Extended Data Fig. 5g).

### Pre-specified confirmation

Five claims selected from exploration were tested once in the 19 held-out districts under criteria dated beforehand (Table 1; Methods). Four were supported after Holm correction: class rates (ρ = 0.71, narrowly above the criterion of 0.7), the finer-scale check (ρ = 0.54), crime profiles (median ρ = 0.85) and the phone-theft clock (P = 0.003). Hubs did not fall significantly less than local streets (log ratio 0.12 against 0.33; P = 0.33), so that pattern remains exploratory. The 2026 hold-out is pending [CONFIRMATION].

## Discussion

Read as a tissue, São Paulo's recorded crime decomposes much as a phenotype does. Composition structures which crimes a place records and at what hour. Persistent differences between places that composition does not explain account for as much of how much crime a tract records, and more where crime concentrates. Within tracts, month-to-month variation exceeds sampling noise mainly where crime is highest, and the models we tested capture little of it.

The noise ceiling can be computed for any city forecasting crime by small area and month, although its value depends on crime type, scale and the handling of events without coordinates. The profiles and rhythms are specific to São Paulo, and associations of crime with the built environment and mobility differ between cities{denadai2020}, but the method needs only a cadastre, a business registry and police records, and the profiles replicated in the 19 held-out districts, with the 2026 data to follow.

The results suggest where and when to look, not where to act. Class rates are averages, and crime concentrates within facility types{eck2007}, so micro-place hot spots remain the unit of action{braga2019}. The profiles and rhythms indicate which components and hours deserve attention: stations, high-rise and mixed commercial parcels for evening phone crime, and, as an exploratory pattern, low-standard residential areas before dawn. If confirmed, the second would justify testing measures that raise guardianship at that hour, such as street lighting{chalfin2022}. Business openings and closures were not followed by detectable changes within a year, although changes of up to about 8% cannot be excluded.

Monthly forecasts improved probabilistic scores by about 6% but added about one percentage point to the share of crime captured in development, so at the scale of tract-months most of what predictive policing{mohler2015} offers for flagging places is already in the map of past crime, a comparison early-warning systems have faced{gorr2015}. That map carries risks: victims report phone crime unevenly{akpinar2021,builgil2021}, flagging by density here under-weighted the poorest tracts relative to their recorded crime, and for offences discovered by police, flags can feed back into where police look{lum2016}.

The analogy earns its place where it yields an analysis urban methods would not have run together, and it fails where it does not (Extended Data Figs. 6–9). Its steps have classical counterparts: Poisson ecological regression{goodman1953}, rate–composition decomposition{kitagawa1955} and time-specific crime pattern analysis{haberman2015,vansleeuwen2021}. What the omics workflow added is their systematic use across all classes and crime types with a positive control, the separation of persistent effects from composition and discipline about sampling noise. It loses on ligand–receptor-style communication and interaction terms; parcel typing predicts no better than raw attributes, and its commercial distinctions are the least stable. It is useful as an instrument that can fail its tests.

The profiles describe recorded crime attributed to classes of places, not traits of their residents. The periphery of São Paulo has long concentrated lethal violence{ceccato2007}, class-level profiles of favelas and low-standard housing could feed territorial stigma, and the favela profiles rest on constructed exposure, although they did not change under five alternative constructions. They should not justify area-wide policing.

The study has limitations. Police records undercount crime unevenly across places, incomes and crime types, which could affect the near-zero residential rates, the decline and the income profile of flagged areas. Coordinates are suppressed more often at homes and stations, and the residential and lethal-violence profiles depend on reconciling those events within districts. The ambient-population survey covers weekdays in 2023–2024 and omits short walks. Attribution is associational and assumes one rate per class, which holds for the ordering of classes but not for their absolute rates, and classes with little crime or correlated exposures, such as low-standard houses with and without a registered business, are barely identified. Parcel typing depends on the attribute table and was not reproducible across its versions (Supplementary Methods). The analysis covers one city; most results come from exploration districts, the confirmatory test covers only 19, and the 2026 hold-out is pending [CONFIRMATION].

## Methods

### Study design and safeguards

The analysis plan (version 0.2, 15 October 2026), its dated amendments and the hashes of frozen inputs and models are version-controlled and will be registered on the Open Science Framework with the vault access log [OSF DOI]{nosek2018}; Supplementary Table 2 gives the chronology. Records dated 2026 have been sealed since ingestion behind a software key; no model has been fitted or evaluated on them, although monthly district counts for 2026 had been seen on the first author's public crime website, as the plan declares. The forecasting benchmark was run on all 96 districts. A spatial vault of 19 districts, drawn at random afterwards (seed 20261016), is excluded from every analysis of the atlas, except a pooled event-study estimate that, before a street-assignment error was corrected, included segments of vault districts; the version reported here excludes them. Parcel types were fixed without crime data and recomputed with the same procedure after crime results of an earlier typing were known (see Parcel types). The confirmatory claims were selected after the exploration results; their criteria were dated on 27 October 2026 (amendment A11), the test ran once on the same day, and 2026 then serves as a sealed hold-out (January–September) and as a prospective window (November 2026–March 2027). Data scope by analysis: parcel typing and tissue architecture used the whole city without crime data; attribution, profiles, rhythms, ambient population, the persistent effect, the decline, the event study, the noise ceiling and the dispersion test used the 77 exploration districts (21,331 census tracts) over 2022–2025; the forecasting benchmark, capture curves and the income audit (Fig. 6a,d,e and Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5) used all 96 districts over 2023–2025 and are development results whose confirmation is the 2026 hold-out. A dry run of the confirmatory code on 19 exploration districts drawn at random (seed 1) checked the code; its results are not evidence.

### Data

Crime records came from the open microdata of the São Paulo State Secretariat of Public Security{ssp_data} for the municipality of São Paulo, 2022–2025: the main police-report base, the dedicated base of mobile-phone thefts and robberies and the base of lethal violence. Records were deduplicated by police station, year, report number, offence and legal heading, and annual totals matched the official tables. Fiscal parcels (1,651,813 after unifying repeated polygons), the 2012–2025 property-tax cadastres, streets and rail and bus stations came from the municipal geoportal{geosampa}. Business establishments came from the December 2025 snapshot of the federal business registry{cnpj}, linked to parcels by postcode and street number (73.8% of active establishments). Census tracts (27,301), favela areas and tract covariates came from the 2022 census{ibge2022}, and ambient population from the 2023 Origin–Destination survey{metro2023}, with the 2017 edition{metro2017} as a sensitivity check.

### Glossary

Parcel type: one of 16 groups of parcels by cadastral and registry attributes. Parcel family: a coarser grouping of the cadastral use codes used for transitions over time. Cell class: one of 23 exposure classes (16 types, favelas, parcels without cadastral record, arterial, collector and local streets, rail stations and bus terminals). Tissue: the composition of a tract in cell classes. Phenotype: the recorded crime of a tract, which reflects its composition (type) and a persistent effect of place (state). Crime type (phenotype): one of the 15 offence groups below. Phone crime: thefts plus robberies of mobile phones. Hubs: rail stations, bus terminals and the commercial and service types. Low-standard housing: the low-rise residential types with low building standard in the cadastre, with or without a registered business (a building attribute, not household income). Registered business: an establishment of the federal registry linked to the parcel by address, including individual micro-entrepreneurs.

### Units and reconciliation

Events with coordinates were assigned to the census tract containing them, and the remaining events to districts through their neighbourhood or police station. Within each district, month and place-type stratum (public road, rail, residence, bus, commerce, other), events without coordinates were distributed among tracts in proportion to the geocoded events of the same stratum over the preceding 12 months (for 2022, over the available months, and by population where none existed), giving fractional counts that sum exactly to district totals. Rail-stratum events were distributed only among tracts within 250 m of a station. In 2025, 17% of phone crime in exploration tracts was reconciled rather than geocoded; residential theft and robbery (19% geocoded) and lethal violence (66%) depend most on reconciliation. Attribution, profiles, the persistent effect, the decline, the noise ceiling and the benchmark use reconciled counts; the finer-scale check, the clock, the risk per pedestrian and the event study use geocoded events only. Because reconciled counts are expectations, we propagated allocation uncertainty with 20 multinomial imputations that redistribute each district, stratum and month total with the same shares; class rates (median imputation interval 4.5% of the district-bootstrap interval), D² (0.44) and the persistent component (0.45) were unchanged. We also hid the coordinates of geocoded events at each stratum's observed missing rate, at random within strata or increasing with tract crime, and reconciled them (10 replicates).

### Parcel types and tissue architecture

Each parcel received 30 attributes: 18 from the cadastre (land and built area, units, floors, land and building values per m², frontage, floor-area ratio, shares of residential, commercial, service, industrial and vacant use, three building standards, corner position and age) and 12 from the business registry (counts in 11 activity groups and of individual micro-entrepreneurs). Counts and areas were log-transformed, all attributes were clipped at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles and standardized, and after principal component analysis (15 components, 82% of variance) we clustered parcels with Leiden{traag2019} on an exact k-nearest-neighbour graph, which makes the labels reproducible. The resolution was chosen by stability: labels from eight subsamples of 80,000 parcels were transferred to 40,000 common parcels and compared by ARI, and we took the most stable resolution with 10 to 16 types (median 16 clusters across subsamples, ARI = 0.95). On a reference sample of 80,000 parcels stratified by district, it gave 17 clusters; two with less than 0.3% of parcels were merged into the nearest centroid, labels were transferred to all parcels by k-nearest neighbours, and parcels with at least four floors or eight units were separated from every type by rule, giving 16 types (ARI = 0.98 between subsamples for the final types; agreement per type in Supplementary Methods). Types were named by rules on their mean profiles, and types sharing a name were qualified by the attribute in which they most exceed the largest type of that name; a geodemographic k-means baseline{gale2016} with the same number of clusters was compared by ARI. An earlier typing, computed before repeated parcel polygons were unified, did not reproduce on the current table (ARI = 0.58; Supplementary Methods). Favelas entered with exposure equal to their informal households times the median frontage per unit of low-rise houses (7.0 m). Neighbourhood enrichment, tissue domains and annual transitions between parcel families are described in Supplementary Methods{palla2022}.

### Composition-aware attribution

For tract s with exposure E~sk~ to class k (km of frontage for parcels and favelas, km of street for the three street classes, counts for stations and terminals), we modelled y~s~ ~ Poisson(Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~) and estimated λ by multiplicative expectation–maximization updates (initialized at the city rate per unit of exposure and iterated until the relative change in log-likelihood fell below 10⁻⁸), which keep rates non-negative. The model assumes one rate per class across the city, additive contributions of classes and exposure proportional to frontage, street length or station count; replacing frontage by lot or built area left the ranking of phone-crime shares nearly unchanged (ρ = 0.97–0.98), and favela profiles were unchanged under exposures based on area, perimeter or households (median ρ of shares ≥ 0.999). Identification was checked by refitting 200 simulated data sets with known rates (Extended Data Fig. 10a); the Poisson design is well conditioned (scaled condition number 6.2). To probe the first assumption we refitted y~s~ ~ Poisson(θ~d~ Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~) with a risk level θ~d~ per district, alternating between λ and θ. Intervals come from 500 bootstrap resamples of districts. D² is the deviance explained relative to a constant expected count across tracts. For the finer-scale check, each geocoded event within 100 m of a station was linked to it and every other event to the nearest street segment within 60 m, whose count was split in proportion to frontage among the parcels within 40 m of the segment; the statistic is the Spearman correlation between attributed and observed rates across parcel types (Fig. 3c also shows it across all cell classes). Because the same geocoded events enter the reconciled counts, this is a consistency check between scales.

### Crime types and profiles

The 15 crime types were phone theft, phone robbery, other street theft, other street robbery (both from pedestrians), vehicle theft, vehicle robbery, theft from vehicles, cargo theft and robbery, theft and robbery on public transport, in commercial premises and in residences, theft of wires and cables, assault outside the home, traffic casualties (injuries and deaths) and lethal violence (intentional homicide, attempted homicide, robbery followed by death and bodily injury followed by death; deaths from police intervention are recorded under other headings and are not included). Reports in the phone base were removed from the main base by report key to avoid double counting, and offences initiated by police, domestic violence and sexual violence were excluded. The share of crime type f attributed to class k is λ~kf~ Σ~s~ E~sk~ divided by its sum over k, and enrichment is the log₂ ratio of this share to its mean over crime types, akin to a location quotient of crime{andresen2007}. Enriched classes were reported when they held at least 2% of the type and the bootstrap interval excluded zero; across 345 class-by-type cells these calls are exploratory. Programs came from non-negative matrix factorization of the tract-by-type matrix scaled by type means{kotliar2019}, a non-negative counterpart of the shared components of multivariate crime models{quick2018}, with ten seeds per rank; stability is the mean cosine similarity of components matched between seeds by the Hungarian algorithm, and we chose the smallest rank of at least three with stability of at least 0.95 (k = 4).

### Tissue clock

Events were assigned to four periods (00:00–05:59, 06:00–11:59, 12:00–17:59, 18:00–23:59) from the exact hour or, when only the period was recorded, from the period (coverage above 99%). Rates λ~k~ were estimated per period, and the contrast H is the exposure-weighted chi-square distance between the period profile of each class and the weighted mean profile, summed over classes. The null permuted periods among events within districts (200 permutations, so P ≤ 0.005 is the floor; 999 for confirmation); block nulls permuted period labels among equal-sized blocks of events of the same tract, period and day (or month) within districts (999 permutations). Four crime types were tested (phone theft, phone robbery, other street robbery and traffic casualties). Hourly profiles used events with exact hour in 2024–2025. The model-free check counted weekday robberies with exact hour in tracts where low-standard houses, or commercial, high-rise and service types, make up at least half of the parcel frontage, a definition by composition that does not depend on the fitted rates, with intervals from 500 bootstrap resamples of districts; 19% of exact hours between 04:00 and 06:00 fall on the full hour, against 26% overall, so heaping does not drive the pre-dawn excess.

### Ambient population

From the 2023 Origin–Destination survey (143,038 records; fieldwork August 2023 to May 2024, weekdays only), we built for every person a timeline of where they were at each hour, weighted by the person expansion factor, and for every trip the minutes spent walking at its origin and destination, weighted by the trip expansion factor; walking and cycling trips counted entirely as time on the street (cycling adds 2.7% of street person-hours, and walking alone gives the same results). The survey omits walks shorter than 500 m except to work or school. Both quantities were rasterized on a 100 m grid, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 300 m) and summed per tract and hour. The rate per pedestrian of a class is its rate per km divided by the exposure-weighted mean pedestrian density of the class; the citywide hourly risk per pedestrian divides geocoded weekday robberies (2024–2025) by the summed pedestrian density of tracts in each hour. Intervals come from 1,000 resamples of households within survey zones, the sampling strata, with crime counts fixed. With the 2017 survey (183,092 records), ρ = 0.69, the median correlation is 0.03 and the 00:00–05:59 risk is 5.4 times the daily mean.

### Persistent effect of place

For 15,929 exploration tracts with at least one expected event per year, let ℓ~st~ be the log annual count (plus 0.5) and m~st~ the log count expected from composition. The composition share is the variance of m~st~, the persistent component is the mean covariance of residuals ℓ~st~ − m~st~ between different years, the transient part is the remaining residual variance, and a cross-term (−11%) absorbs the covariance between composition and residual; all are fractions of the variance of ℓ~st~. Tracts below one expected event per year were excluded because their log counts are dominated by zeros. Out-of-sample R² values used cross-validation blocked by district.

### Decline and business openings

Class rates were estimated separately for each year with exposure fixed at 2025, for phone crime and separately for phone theft and phone robbery. The relative ratio of a class is its 2025-to-2022 rate ratio divided by that of the city for the same crime, with district-bootstrap intervals. The event study compared street crime on segments after their first opening or closure of a bar, restaurant or shop in 2023–2024 with never-treated segments of the same district and street class, a difference-in-differences with never-treated controls (cf. ref.{callaway2021}; design in Supplementary Methods); 80% power corresponds to effects of 5–7% (9% for the strict variants).

### Noise ceiling and dispersion

For counts aggregated to a given space-time scale, the noise ceiling is 1 − mean/variance, the share of variance above Poisson noise and thus the largest R² any forecast can reach; within units, the same quantity uses the variance around each unit's mean. The dispersion test for tract s compares Σ~t~ (y~st~ − ȳ~s~)²/ȳ~s~ over the 12 months of 2025 with a χ² distribution on 11 degrees of freedom at α = 0.05{cochran1954}, in the 16,432 exploration tracts with at least one event; shares of tracts are descriptive, without multiplicity correction. Null rejection rates were simulated (200 replicates) by drawing Poisson events per tract, stratum and month at constant 2025 rates, geocoding each with the observed district–stratum share and reconciling the rest as in production. Power was simulated with gamma-distributed monthly rates: tracts with 0.25 events per month, the order of the median tract, and a monthly coefficient of variation of 1 are flagged 12% of the time. The common relative overdispersion was estimated by moments as Σ~s~(SS~s~ − 11ȳ~s~)/(11Σ~s~ ȳ~s~²), where SS~s~ is the sum of squared deviations, and the expected share of excess variance in the top 1% of tracts is then their share of Σ ȳ~s~².

### Forecasting benchmark

The benchmark predicted phone crime per tract and month one month ahead, fitted on 2023–2024 and evaluated on 2025, with features restricted to information available before each month; models, inputs, tuning and temporal availability are in Supplementary Table 1. Baselines were a negative binomial generalized linear model, the historical mean, persistence, seasonal naïve, kernel density and prospective hot-spotting; other methods were a self-exciting point process{mohler2011}, gradient boosting, a spatio-temporal graph neural network, geographically weighted and spatial-panel regressions, Bayesian disease-mapping models{riebler2016}, a zero-shot time-series foundation model and a species-distribution model, plus a stacked ensemble. All forecasts were negative binomial and scored against the reconciled count by the continuous ranked probability score{gneiting2007}, integrated exactly for real-valued counts (an earlier implementation scored a fractional count as the next integer; the correction is an amendment pending registration), the log score of the rounded count, the Brier score of P(Y ≥ 1) against a count of at least 0.5 and the share of crime captured in 1% and 5% of the area. Differences used 2,000 bootstrap resamples of districts and Holm correction{holm1979}. Lethal violence was modelled at district by quarter. A pre-specified language-model decision layer was outperformed by gradient boosting on the same compressed state (Brier score 0.1084 against 0.1079). Calibration within income quintiles is the ratio of predicted to observed counts.

### Statistics

Unless stated, intervals are 95% percentile intervals from district bootstrap resamples, ρ is Spearman's correlation across classes, r is Pearson's correlation and P values are two-sided. The confirmatory family (C1–C5) uses one-sided tests with Holm correction and the criteria of Table 1, dated in amendment A11. The benchmark hypothesis H1 is that the best cross-domain model has a lower CRPS than the negative binomial baseline in 2026.

### Privacy and ethics

The study uses public administrative records and a public survey without contact with people. Report numbers, addresses and coordinates stayed on a local machine; outputs are aggregated to tracts or classes, tract-level crime maps are rasterized, and the public explorer shows no per-tract risk. Class profiles are not intended for area-wide policing or for inferring the characteristics of residents. [ETHICS: institutional review statement and any stakeholder consultation.]
