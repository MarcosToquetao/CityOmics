## Abstract

Crime concentrates at few places, but which components of a place host which crimes, and when, is hard to measure without coordinates. We read São Paulo as a spatially resolved tissue: 1.65 million fiscal parcels are cells, census tracts are units of known composition and 0.69 million police records cover 15 crime types. A composition-aware model gives each class of cell a distinct crime profile, robust to district risk and reproduced by geocoded events (median Spearman ρ = 0.85). Daily rhythms of phone theft differ by class and replicate across years; robbery rhythms do not follow pedestrian flows, with an exploratory pre-dawn excess on low-standard housing. A persistent place effect beyond composition explains more of how much crime a tract records. Month-to-month variation in phone crime within tracts is real but too sparse to detect or forecast, so forecasts add little to a stable map of past crime.

<!--MAIN-->
Crime is concentrated at a small share of addresses and street segments, and the same places tend to stay at the top for years{sherman1989,weisburd2004,weisburd2015}. The pattern holds in Latin American cities{chainey2019,demelo2015}. Its explanations concern what places contain and when people use them: routine activities bring offenders and targets together where capable guardians are absent{cohen1979,hollispeel2011}, some land uses generate or attract crime{brantingham1995,bernasco2011} and a few facilities of each type account for most of its crime{eck2007}. Facility and land-use studies estimate these effects for a few uses or crime types at a time{twinam2017,andresen2017}, risk terrain models combine many layers into a single risk surface{caplan2011}, and evidence on whether the effect of a place changes with the hour is mixed{haberman2015,bernasco2017,vansleeuwen2021}. Two gaps remain. Few studies read the full composition of a city, all its crime types and the hour together. And in many cities police records lack usable coordinates: in São Paulo, 17% of phone crime does, rising to 82% on the rail network, as do 81% of residential thefts and robberies, so the contribution of each component must be inferred from areal counts, the problem of ecological inference{robinson1950,goodman1953}.

Spatial transcriptomics faces the same problem. Its measurement units, called spots, capture mixtures of cells{moses2022}. Given reference profiles of cell types, deconvolution estimates the composition of each spot{kleshchevnikov2022,cable2022}. When composition is known, the same model run the other way estimates the profile of each type{shenorr2010,newman2019}. Around this core the field measures profiles over many outputs at once{kotliar2019}, rhythms that differ between tissues{zhang2014}, cell states beyond type and the sampling-noise floor of sparse counts{sarkar2021}. Urban research has borrowed from biology before, from the human ecology of the Chicago school{park1925,shaw1942} and the DNA of neighbourhoods{delmelle2016}, mostly as a framing rather than as a measurement instrument whose outputs can fail tests.

Here we use this toolkit as an instrument on São Paulo, a city of 11.5 million people. Fiscal parcels are cells, census tracts are spots of known composition and crime is a phenotype measured over the tissue (Fig. 1a). We ask three questions: which crimes each class of cell hosts, at what hour, and what remains to be predicted once composition and history are known. Parcel types were fixed without crime data, and all analyses of the atlas use 77 exploration districts, holding out 19 districts for a single confirmatory test (Methods). We find that composition structures which crimes a place hosts and when, that a persistent effect of place beyond composition accounts for more of how much crime it records, and that month-to-month variation of phone crime within tracts is too sparse to detect or forecast.

## Results

### A city read as a tissue

We described each of 1,651,813 fiscal parcels by 30 attributes from the 2025 property-tax cadastre and the business registry, covering land use, building standard and height, frontage and the activities of businesses linked to the parcel by address. Clustering these attributes with the Leiden algorithm{traag2019}, without crime data, gave 12 parcel types that were stable across subsamples (adjusted Rand index (ARI) = 0.973 at the selected resolution) and agreed moderately with a geodemographic k-means baseline{gale2016} (ARI = 0.71; Fig. 1b and Fig. 2a,b,f). Census favelas, whose informal homes are absent from the cadastre, entered as 3,879 area units with constructed exposure. Tracts mix many types (Fig. 2c), and types are spatially organized into neighbourhoods and contiguous domains (Fig. 2d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1). With favelas, parcels lacking a cadastral record, three classes of street, rail stations and bus terminals, the 12 types form 19 cell classes (glossary in Methods). The composition of a tract in these classes is its tissue.

### Each cell class has a crime profile

We modelled the crime count of each tract as the sum of what is attributed to its cells: a Poisson model whose expectation is Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~, where E~sk~ is the exposure of class k in tract s (metres of frontage or of street, or number of stations) and λ~k~ is the rate of the class (Methods). This is Poisson ecological regression with known exposures{goodman1953}, the urban counterpart of estimating type-specific profiles from mixtures of known composition{shenorr2010}. For phone crime (thefts and robberies of mobile phones) in 2025, 121,434 events in exploration tracts, composition alone explained a third of the deviance relative to a constant count per tract (D² = 0.34) and assigned rates spanning four orders of magnitude (Fig. 3a): 71 events per km of frontage per year on middle-standard street retail (95% interval 48–96), 37 on high-rise parcels, 87 per rail station and close to zero on low-rise residential parcels. The ranking is robust to district-level risk: allowing a multiplicative risk level per district preserved the order of classes (Spearman ρ = 0.96), although it roughly halved the rates of central classes such as middle-standard retail (23 per km) and stations (45 per station). Re-allocating the 100,347 geocoded events to the nearest street segment and its abutting parcels reproduced the ranking of parcel types (ρ = 0.93), a consistency check at finer scale rather than a test on independent data.

We then extended the outcome to 15 crime types (0.69 million events in 2024–2025; Extended Data Fig. 2a) and fitted them jointly. The resulting class-by-crime profiles behave like reference signatures (Fig. 3b): the most enriched class differs between crime types, with 11 distinct classes at the top. Cargo theft and robbery is enriched on industrial parcels and favelas (log₂ enrichment +2.0 and +1.9), lethal violence, 66% geocoded, on low-standard houses, vacant land and favelas (+1.5, +1.5 and +1.2), and phone theft at rail stations (+1.6; not tested at finer scale). Crime types defined by place behave as positive controls: theft and robbery on public transport are enriched at terminals and stations, residential theft and robbery on middle-standard houses, and traffic casualties have their highest rate among streets on arterials (1.50 against 0.62 per km on local streets). The profiles survive district-level risk (median ρ = 0.89 between the shares of each crime type with and without district effects; same top class for 10 of 15 types). The finer-scale check held in the 12 crime types with at least 80% of events geocoded (median ρ = 0.85; Fig. 3c), from 0.93–0.95 for other street theft, phone robbery and vehicle theft to 0.62–0.74 for cargo theft and robbery, vehicle robbery and theft from vehicles. Factorizing the tract-by-crime matrix{kotliar2019} gave four programs, stable across seeds, that read as transport, dense-centre, peripheral-logistics and residential crime (Fig. 3d,e). Composition thus structures not only how much crime a tract hosts but which crime.

### Each class keeps its own daily rhythm

Classes also differ in when crime occurs (Fig. 4a). We call this the tissue clock: a class-specific daily profile, not an endogenous oscillator. We measured how far the period-of-day profile of each class departs from that of the city and compared it with a null that permutes periods within districts. For phone theft, the pre-specified test, class-specific departures exceeded the null in 2024 and 2025 (P ≤ 0.005 in each year) and replicated between years (r = 0.93; Fig. 4d). Phone robbery departed from the null in 2024 (P ≤ 0.005) but not in 2025 (P = 0.07), with replication r = 0.90; other street robbery and traffic casualties did not replicate. Robbery on retail, high-rise and service parcels peaks between 19:00 and 22:00, and at rail stations at 18:00. On low-standard housing the model places a large pre-dawn peak (Fig. 4b). A model-free check confirms the direction but not the size: in the 313 tracts where low-standard housing dominates expected robbery, 16% of weekday robberies with an exact hour occurred between 04:00 and 06:00 (95% interval 12–21%), against 6% in tracts dominated by commerce.

A rhythm of this kind could simply track the number of people outside. We estimated the hourly ambient population{hipp2019} of every tract from the 2017 Origin–Destination household survey{metro2017}, separating people present from people walking on the street (Methods). The ordering of classes changes little when rates are divided by pedestrians (Spearman ρ = 0.75 against rates per km; Fig. 4f), a weak check because pedestrian density varies across classes far less than crime rates (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The changes are informative: industrial parcels, with few pedestrians, rise in the per-pedestrian ranking, as crime attractors do, while high-rise parcels fall, as generators do{brantingham1995,bernasco2011}. The rhythm, however, does not follow the crowd. Across classes, the median correlation between the hourly profiles of robbery and of pedestrians is 0.03, and citywide robbery risk per pedestrian peaks at 03:00, at 19 times its daily mean (Fig. 4c). Robbery on low-standard housing is 0.4 times as frequent per day at weekends as on weekdays (Fig. 4e), when fewer residents commute. This pattern is consistent with low guardianship and with offenders converging on commuters' paths to transit{cohen1979}; target suitability, darkness{tompson2013} and offender routines remain untested alternatives, and robbers' choice of blocks has been found to vary little with the hour elsewhere{bernasco2017}.

### Type and state: a persistent effect of place

Composition is not all that a tract carries (Fig. 5). Out of sample, tissue features without crime history predicted the 2025 tract rate with R² = 0.25 (0.31 with tract covariates), against 0.48 for the tract's historical mean (Fig. 5a). Parcel types (R² = 0.22) did not outperform the same attributes without typing (0.26), so typing serves attribution and interpretation rather than prediction. Splitting the between-tract variance of log annual rates over 2022–2025, composition accounted for 42% and a component that persists across years beyond composition for 48%, with a cross-term of −13% for their covariance; in the 3,626 tracts with at least 10 expected events per year, the split was 18% against 58% (Fig. 5b). The persistent component held with geocoded events only (40% against 46%), and residuals remained correlated at a lag of three years (r = 0.63; Fig. 5c). The tissue itself barely changes: a classifier of parcel families keeps 98.2% of parcels in the same family from one year to the next in the cadastres of 2012–2025 (Fig. 5d), an upper bound given classifier error.

In omics terms this component is a cell state beyond type. In criminological terms it is persistent heterogeneity between places, which covariances across years cannot separate from state dependence{nagin2000}. Candidates include uses the cadastre misses (street vending, informal commerce, unlinked businesses), place management{eck2007} and territorial control by organized crime{biderman2019}. Typing describes what a place is. Where crime concentrates most, its history says more.

### Rates move within a stable tissue

Phone crime fell by 20% between 2022 and 2025 in exploration tracts, through robbery (−40%) rather than theft (−2%). We estimated class rates by year with composition fixed at 2025 (Fig. 5e for phone crime as a whole). Because classes differ in their mix of theft and robbery (robbery is 98% of phone crime on low-standard housing and 28% at stations), we compared each class with the city within each crime. Phone robbery fell faster than the city's robbery on low-standard housing (relative ratio 0.56, 95% interval 0.34–0.78) and corner housing (0.49), whereas middle-standard retail (1.15, 0.90–1.52) and rail stations (0.91, 0.69–1.19) kept pace with or fell less than the city. The fall of robbery was therefore spread across the residential fabric rather than concentrated at hubs, unlike the micro-place crime drops in Seattle and Boston, which came from few segments{weisburd2004,braga2011}. The two studies differ in unit, crime and period. Candidate drivers include the national phone-blocking scheme launched in December 2023{celularseguro}, policing and changes in reporting, none of which we test. Other crime types moved in both directions (Fig. 5f). Single business openings and closures on street segments did not change street crime within 12 months: in a staggered event study{callaway2021}, effects outside −14% to +5% of the baseline rate are excluded in every variant (Extended Data Fig. 3).

### Month-to-month variation is sparse and hard to forecast

If composition and history account for most of where crime occurs, what remains is when, month to month. Flagging the 5% of the area at highest forecast risk, an ensemble of forecasting models captured 53% of 2025 phone crime, as much as the historical mean (52%) and the share expected if the ensemble's forecasts were exact (52%), against 65% for an ex-post oracle (Fig. 6a). We compared explained variance with a noise ceiling, the largest R² any model could reach given Poisson noise{cochran1954} (Methods). Between and within tracts together, the ensemble reached R² = 0.80 of a ceiling of 0.92 for tract-months, and 87–99% of the ceiling at every scale (Fig. 6b). Within tracts, it explained R² = 0.018 of a ceiling of 0.56: real month-to-month variation exists, but the models do not capture it.

The variation is sparse rather than absent. The median exploration tract recorded 0.19 phone crimes per month, and a per-tract dispersion test rejected Poisson noise in only 4% of tracts (14% of tracts with at least one event per month; Fig. 6c). With geocoded events only it rejected in 7%, so reconciling events without coordinates smooths monthly counts and these shares are lower bounds. At such counts the test has little power: simulated tracts with 0.25 events per month and a month-to-month coefficient of variation of 1 are flagged 12% of the time. Assuming the same relative overdispersion everywhere (a latent monthly coefficient of variation of about 0.33) reproduces the concentration of extra-Poisson variance in the 1% of tracts with most crime (88% expected, 86% observed). Month-to-month variation is thus consistent with a property shared by all tracts that only high-count tracts make visible, much as Poisson measurement noise masks variation in expression at low counts in single cells{sarkar2021}. These statements concern phone crime at the scale of tract-months. Near-repeat excitation operates over days and hundreds of metres{johnson2007} and is visible here between parcel families (Knox ratio 1.12 on average and 1.68 within favelas; Extended Data Fig. 9a), but it averages out within a month and a tract.

Forecasting methods from seismology, epidemiology, ecology, econometrics and machine learning did little better than simple baselines. In a benchmark on 2025, run in development, the best model improved the continuous ranked probability score{gneiting2007} over a negative binomial generalized linear model by 4% (difference −0.025, 95% interval −0.034 to −0.016; Fig. 6d; Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5) but did not raise the share of crime captured in 1% of the area. For lethal violence at district scale, no model improved on the negative binomial baseline (Fig. 6e). Forecasts were calibrated within 5% across tract income quintiles, yet flagging by density placed 36% of the flagged area in the richest quintile and 3% in the poorest, which recorded 26% and 7% of phone crime (Fig. 6f).

## Discussion

Read as a tissue, São Paulo's recorded crime behaves like a phenotype in the biological sense: it reflects both what a place is and the state it is in. Composition structures which crimes a place hosts and at what hour. A persistent effect of place beyond composition accounts for more of how much crime a tract records, especially where crime concentrates. Within tracts, month-to-month variation is real but too sparse to detect or forecast. These statements were checked against data the main model did not use where possible: a second year for the rhythm, a model-free count for pre-dawn robbery and survey-based pedestrian estimates for the crowd.

Some of these results should travel and some should not. The noise ceiling is a property of counts: any city that forecasts crime by small area and month can compute it, although its value depends on the crime type and the scale. The profiles and rhythms are specific to São Paulo, but the method needs only a cadastre, a business registry and police records, which many cities publish, and the 19 held-out districts and the 2026 data test how far they replicate.

The results suggest where and when to look, not where to act. Class rates are averages, and crime concentrates within facility types{eck2007}, so micro-place hot spots remain the unit of action{braga2019}. The profiles and rhythms indicate which components and hours deserve attention: transit hubs and middle-standard retail for evening phone crime, and the routes from low-standard housing to transit before dawn. The second pattern suggests testing measures that raise guardianship at those hours, such as street lighting{chalfin2022} and staffed bus stops, and it involves transit operators and municipal services as much as the police. The null event study suggests that single business openings or closures are not a lever within a year.

The results also bound the value of forecasting. Monthly forecasts add little to a stable map of past crime, so most of the value of predictive policing{mohler2015} lies in that map. Its use carries risks in both directions. Victims report phone crime unevenly, which biases the map itself{akpinar2021,builgil2021}; flagging by density here under-weighted the poorest tracts relative to their recorded crime; and for offences discovered by police, flags can feed back into where police look{lum2016}.

The analogy earns its place where it yields an analysis urban methods would not have run together, and it fails where it does not (Extended Data Figs. 6–9). Its steps have classical counterparts: Poisson ecological regression{goodman1953}, rate–composition decomposition{kitagawa1955} and time-specific crime pattern analysis{haberman2015,vansleeuwen2021}. What the omics workflow added is their systematic use across all classes and crime types with a positive control, the separation of state from type and a discipline about sampling noise. It loses on ligand–receptor-style communication and interaction terms, and parcel typing predicts no better than raw attributes. It is useful as an instrument that can fail its tests.

The profiles describe recorded crime attributed to classes of places, not traits of their residents. The periphery of São Paulo has long concentrated lethal violence{ceccato2007}, class-level profiles of favelas and low-standard housing could feed territorial stigma, and the favela profiles rest partly on constructed exposure. They should not justify area-wide policing.

The study has limitations. Police records undercount crime unevenly across places, incomes and crime types; reporting may differ between theft and robbery and with income, which could affect the near-zero residential rates, the decline and the income profile of flagged areas, where the recorded 26% against 7% may overstate the share of richer tracts. Coordinates are suppressed more often at homes and stations, and the residential and lethal-violence profiles depend on reconciling those events within districts. The ambient-population survey dates from 2017 and covers weekdays. Attribution is associational and assumes one rate per class, which holds for the ordering of classes but not for their absolute rates. Several robustness checks remain: a held-out split of geocoded events for the finer-scale check, block permutations for the clock, a hierarchical model of monthly overdispersion and the sensitivity of favela profiles to their constructed exposure. The analysis covers one city, and the results reported here come from exploration districts; the confirmatory test is pending [CONFIRMATION].

## Methods

### Study design and safeguards

The analysis plan, its amendments and the commit hashes of frozen models are version-controlled; registration on the Open Science Framework is planned before the held-out data are opened [OSF DOI]{nosek2018}. A temporal vault seals all records with an event date in 2026 behind a software key. A spatial vault, defined after the forecasting benchmark had been run, holds out 19 of the 96 districts from every analysis of the atlas. Data scope by analysis: parcel typing and tissue architecture used the whole city without crime data; attribution, profiles, rhythms, ambient population, the persistent effect, the decline, the event study, the noise ceiling and the dispersion test used the 77 exploration districts (21,331 census tracts) over 2022–2025; the forecasting benchmark, capture curves and the income audit (Fig. 6a,d,e,f and Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5) used all 96 districts over 2023–2025 and are development results whose confirmation is the 2026 hold-out. No crime result of the atlas has been computed in the vault districts. A dry run of the confirmatory code on 19 exploration districts drawn at random (seed 1) checked the code; its results are not evidence.

### Data

Crime records came from the open microdata of the São Paulo State Secretariat of Public Security{ssp_data} for the municipality of São Paulo, 2022–2025: the main police-report base, the dedicated base of mobile-phone thefts and robberies and the base of lethal violence. Records were deduplicated by police station, year, report number, offence and legal heading, and annual totals matched the official tables. Fiscal parcels (1,651,813 after unifying repeated polygons), the 2012–2025 property-tax cadastres, streets and rail and bus stations came from the municipal geoportal{geosampa}. Business establishments came from the December 2025 snapshot of the federal business registry{cnpj}, linked to parcels by postcode and street number (73.8% of active establishments). Census tracts (27,301), favela areas and tract covariates came from the 2022 census{ibge2022}, and ambient population from the 2017 Origin–Destination survey{metro2017}.

### Glossary

Parcel type: one of 12 clusters of parcels by cadastral and registry attributes. Parcel family: a coarser grouping of the cadastral use codes used for transitions over time. Cell class: one of 19 exposure classes (12 types, favelas, parcels without cadastral record, arterial, collector and local streets, rail stations and bus terminals). Tissue: the composition of a tract in cell classes. Phenotype: the recorded crime of a tract, which reflects its composition (type) and a persistent effect of place (state). Crime type (phenotype): one of the 15 offence groups below. Phone crime: thefts plus robberies of mobile phones. Hubs: rail stations, bus terminals and commercial classes. Low-standard housing: the low-rise residential type with low building standard in the cadastre (a building attribute, not household income).

### Units and reconciliation

Events with coordinates were assigned to the census tract containing them, and the remaining events to districts through their neighbourhood or police station. Within each district, month and place-type stratum (public road, rail, residence, bus, commerce, other), events without coordinates were distributed among tracts in proportion to the geocoded events of the same stratum over the preceding 12 months (for 2022, over the available months, and by population where none existed), giving fractional counts that sum exactly to district totals. Rail-stratum events were distributed only among tracts within 250 m of a station. In 2025, 17% of phone crime in exploration tracts was reconciled rather than geocoded; residential theft and robbery (19% geocoded) and lethal violence (66%) depend most on reconciliation. Attribution uses reconciled counts; the finer-scale check, the clock's model-free count and the geocoded-only sensitivities use geocoded events.

### Parcel types and tissue architecture

Each parcel received 30 attributes: 18 from the cadastre (land and built area, units, floors, land and building values per m², frontage, floor-area ratio, shares of residential, commercial, service, industrial and vacant use, three building standards, corner position and age) and 12 from the business registry (counts in 11 activity groups and of individual micro-entrepreneurs). Counts and areas were log-transformed, all attributes were clipped at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles and standardized, and after principal component analysis (15 components, 90% of variance) we clustered parcels with Leiden{traag2019}. The resolution was chosen by stability: labels from eight subsamples of 80,000 parcels were transferred to 40,000 common parcels and compared by ARI. The chosen resolution (14 clusters, ARI = 0.973) was refined by merging four clusters with less than 0.3% of parcels and by separating high-rise parcels (at least four floors or eight units) from the mixed type, giving 12 types named by rules on their mean profiles. Favelas entered with exposure equal to their informal households times the median frontage of low-standard parcels (7.0 m). Neighbourhood enrichment used a k-nearest-neighbour graph (k = 8) and 100 permutations restricted to district by population-density decile{palla2022}; tissue domains came from smoothing tract composition over contiguous neighbours and k-means (k = 8, chosen by silhouette). Annual transitions between parcel families came from a gradient-boosting classifier of family from cadastral attributes (93.6% accuracy on held-out parcels), applied to the cadastres of 2012–2025; pairs involving 2021 were excluded because cadastral coverage drops by about 25,000 parcels that year.

### Composition-aware attribution

For tract s with exposure E~sk~ to class k (km of frontage for parcels and favelas, km of street for the three street classes, counts for stations and terminals), we modelled y~s~ ~ Poisson(Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~) and estimated λ by multiplicative expectation–maximization updates (initialized at the city rate per unit of exposure and iterated until the relative change in log-likelihood fell below 10⁻⁸), which keep rates non-negative. The model assumes one rate per class across the city, additive contributions of classes and exposure proportional to frontage, street length or station count. To probe the first assumption we refitted y~s~ ~ Poisson(θ~d~ Σ~k~ E~sk~ λ~k~) with a risk level θ~d~ per district, alternating between λ and θ. "Fitted jointly" means that all crime types share the same exposure matrix and are estimated in one vectorized fit; the likelihood factorizes by crime type. Intervals come from 500 bootstrap resamples of districts. D² is the deviance explained relative to a constant expected count across tracts. For the finer-scale check, each geocoded event within 100 m of a station was linked to it and every other event to the nearest street segment within 60 m, whose count was split in proportion to frontage among the parcels within 40 m of the segment; the statistic is the Spearman correlation between attributed and observed rates across parcel types (Fig. 3c also shows it across all cell classes). Because the same geocoded events enter the reconciled counts, this is a consistency check between scales.

### Crime types and profiles

The 15 crime types were phone theft, phone robbery, other street theft, other street robbery (both from pedestrians), vehicle theft, vehicle robbery, theft from vehicles, cargo theft and robbery, theft and robbery on public transport, in commercial premises and in residences, theft of wires and cables, assault outside the home, traffic casualties (injuries and deaths) and lethal violence (intentional homicide, attempted homicide, robbery followed by death and bodily injury followed by death; deaths from police intervention are recorded under other headings and are not included). Reports in the phone base were removed from the main base by report key to avoid double counting, and offences initiated by police, domestic violence and sexual violence were excluded. The share of crime type f attributed to class k is λ~kf~ Σ~s~ E~sk~ divided by its sum over k, and enrichment is the log₂ ratio of this share to its mean over crime types. Enriched classes were reported when they held at least 2% of the type and the bootstrap interval excluded zero; across 285 class-by-type cells these calls are exploratory. Programs came from non-negative matrix factorization of the tract-by-type matrix scaled by type means{kotliar2019}, with ten seeds per rank; stability is the mean cosine similarity of components matched between seeds by the Hungarian algorithm, and we chose the smallest rank of at least three with stability of at least 0.95 (k = 4).

### Tissue clock

Events were assigned to four periods (00:00–05:59, 06:00–11:59, 12:00–17:59, 18:00–23:59) from the exact hour or, when only the period was recorded, from the period (coverage above 99%). Rates λ~k~ were estimated per period, and the contrast H is the exposure-weighted chi-square distance between the period profile of each class and the weighted mean profile, summed over classes. The null permuted periods among events within districts (200 permutations, so P ≤ 0.005 is the floor; 999 for confirmation). Four crime types were tested (phone theft, phone robbery, other street robbery and traffic casualties). Hourly profiles used events with exact hour in 2024–2025. The model-free check counted weekday robberies with exact hour in tracts where one class holds at least half of the expected robbery, with intervals from 500 bootstrap resamples of events; 19% of exact hours between 04:00 and 06:00 fall on the full hour, against 26% overall, so heaping does not drive the pre-dawn excess.

### Ambient population

From the 2017 Origin–Destination survey (183,092 records), we built for every person a timeline of where they were at each hour, weighted by the person expansion factor, and for every trip the minutes spent walking at its origin and destination, weighted by the trip expansion factor; walking and cycling trips counted entirely as time on the street. Both quantities were rasterized on a 100 m grid, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 300 m) and summed per tract and hour for weekdays. The rate per pedestrian of a class is its rate per km divided by the exposure-weighted mean pedestrian density of the class; the citywide hourly risk per pedestrian divides weekday robberies by pedestrians on the street in each hour.

### Persistent effect of place

For 15,708 exploration tracts with at least one expected event per year, let ℓ~st~ be the log annual count (plus 0.5) and m~st~ the log count expected from composition. The composition share is the variance of m~st~, the persistent component is the mean covariance of residuals ℓ~st~ − m~st~ between different years, the transient part is the remaining residual variance, and a cross-term (−13%) absorbs the covariance between composition and residual; all are fractions of the variance of ℓ~st~. Tracts below one expected event per year were excluded because their log counts are dominated by zeros. Out-of-sample R² values used cross-validation blocked by district.

### Decline and business openings

Class rates were estimated separately for each year with exposure fixed at 2025, for phone crime and separately for phone theft and phone robbery. The relative ratio of a class is its 2025-to-2022 rate ratio divided by that of the city for the same crime, with district-bootstrap intervals. For the event study, openings and closures of bars and restaurants and openings of retail establishments were dated from the registry and linked to street segments, in two variants: any establishment, and only establishments other than individual micro-entrepreneurs on parcels with commercial or service use. The outcome was street crime (phone theft and robbery and other street theft and robbery) per segment-month. Each treated segment was compared with the mean of never-treated segments of the same district and street class, in the spirit of ref.{callaway2021}, using baseline months −12 to −7, a pre-trend window of months −6 to −1 and an effect window of months 0 to 11; intervals come from 200 bootstrap resamples of treated segments clustered by district, with controls fixed, and the excluded range is the union of the 95% intervals relative to baseline.

### Noise ceiling and dispersion

For counts aggregated to a given space-time scale, the noise ceiling is 1 − mean/variance, the share of variance above Poisson noise and thus the largest R² any forecast can reach; within units, the same quantity uses the variance around each unit's mean. The dispersion test for tract s compares Σ~t~ (y~st~ − ȳ~s~)²/ȳ~s~ over the 12 months of 2025 with a χ² distribution on 11 degrees of freedom at α = 0.05{cochran1954}, in the 16,432 exploration tracts with at least one event; we report shares of tracts without multiplicity correction because the test is used descriptively. Power was simulated (4,000 tracts per setting) with gamma-distributed monthly rates. The common relative overdispersion was estimated by moments as Σ~s~(SS~s~ − 11ȳ~s~)/(11Σ~s~ ȳ~s~²), where SS~s~ is the sum of squared deviations, and the expected share of excess variance in the top 1% of tracts is then their share of Σ ȳ~s~².

### Forecasting benchmark

The benchmark predicted phone crime per tract and month one month ahead, fitted on 2023–2024 and evaluated on 2025, with features restricted to information available before each month. Baselines were a negative binomial generalized linear model, the historical mean, persistence, seasonal naïve, kernel density and prospective hot-spotting. Other methods were a self-exciting point process{mohler2011}, gradient boosting with and without crime history, a spatio-temporal graph neural network, geographically weighted and spatial-panel regressions, Bayesian disease-mapping models{riebler2016}, a zero-shot time-series foundation model and a species-distribution model, plus a stacked ensemble weighted on the second half of 2024. All forecasts were negative binomial and scored by the continuous ranked probability score{gneiting2007}, log score, Brier score of at least one event and the share of crime captured in 1% and 5% of the area. Differences used 2,000 bootstrap resamples of districts and Holm correction{holm1979}. Lethal violence was modelled at district by quarter. A pre-specified comparison of a fine-tuned language-model decision layer with gradient boosting on the same compressed state favoured gradient boosting (Brier score 0.1079 against 0.1084). Calibration within income quintiles is the ratio of predicted to observed counts.

### Exploratory analogy audit

Extended Data Figs. 6–9 compare each omics-derived module with an urban analogue on the same data: neighbourhood enrichment against the colocation quotient, criminological ligand–receptor scores (generator and target layers combined within 250–1,000 m and tested by permutation) against bivariate Lee's L, MISTy views against a spatial lag, interaction terms against additive models and near-repeat excitation by the Knox ratio (400 m, 1–14 days). A module wins when it adds out-of-sample skill or a quantity its analogue cannot produce, ties when both give the same reading and loses when the analogue is simpler and no worse.

### Statistics

Unless stated, intervals are 95% percentile intervals from district bootstrap resamples, ρ is Spearman's correlation across classes, r is Pearson's correlation and P values are two-sided. The confirmatory family (C1–C5) uses Holm correction with pre-specified criteria: C1, ρ ≥ 0.7 between exploration and held-out class rates; C2, ρ > 0 for the finer-scale check; C3, median ρ ≥ 0.7 between profile shares; C4, the phone-theft contrast H above its permutation null; C5, a positive difference between the log relative ratios of hubs (stations and middle-standard retail) and local streets, reported also within phone robbery to separate it from the mix of theft and robbery. The benchmark hypothesis H1 is that the best cross-domain model has a lower continuous ranked probability score than the negative binomial baseline in 2026 (Holm-adjusted P < 0.05).
